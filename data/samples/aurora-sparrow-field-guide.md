# Field Guide to the Aurora Sparrow

*Passerella borealis aurorae* — a fictional species written for the doc-rag demo.
Every fact in this file is invented; it exists so you can check whether an
answer is really grounded in the document.

## Identification

The Aurora Sparrow is a medium-sized songbird with a wingspan of 24 centimetres
and an average adult mass of 31 grams. Adults show a slate-grey crown, a rust
coloured nape, and three white bars on each wing. Juveniles lack the rust nape
until their second autumn moult.

The species is most reliably told apart from the similar Harbour Sparrow by its
call: a four-note descending whistle, where the Harbour Sparrow gives three
notes. In poor light, the pale eye-ring of the Aurora Sparrow is the best
field mark.

## Range and Habitat

Aurora Sparrows breed along the Kestrel Coast between the Marrow River delta and
Fenwick Point. The total breeding range covers about 48,000 square kilometres.
Birds winter inland on the Caldera Plateau, between 900 and 1,600 metres above
sea level.

Preferred breeding habitat is open birch scrub with a dense layer of crowberry.
Birds avoid closed-canopy forest entirely, and densities fall sharply where
crowberry cover drops below 30 percent of the ground layer.

## Breeding

Pairs are seasonally monogamous. The female builds a cup nest of grass and
feather down in 5 to 7 days, usually within 40 centimetres of the ground.
Clutch size is 3 to 5 eggs, with a mean of 4.1 eggs across 212 monitored nests.

Incubation lasts 13 days and is performed by the female alone. Both parents feed
the nestlings, which fledge 16 days after hatching. In a good year, roughly 62
percent of nests fledge at least one chick; in the drought year of 2019 that
figure fell to 24 percent.

## Diet

During the breeding season adults take mostly insects, with crane-fly larvae
making up 44 percent of prey items delivered to nestlings. Outside the breeding
season the diet shifts to seeds, especially crowberry and sedge seed.

Adults drink dew from leaves and rarely visit standing water, which makes them
hard to catch at water traps.

## Migration

Departure from the breeding grounds peaks in the second week of September.
Satellite tags fitted to 18 adults in 2021 showed a mean autumn journey of 1,240
kilometres completed in 9 days, with a single stopover in the Thornfield
wetlands. Spring return begins in late March.

## Conservation Status

The Aurora Sparrow is listed as Near Threatened by the fictional Boreal Bird
Panel. The population was estimated at 180,000 mature individuals in the 2022
survey, down 18 percent from the 2012 estimate of 220,000.

The main threats are the loss of birch scrub to commercial plantations and nest
predation by introduced stoats. A nest-protection trial on Fenwick Point raised
fledging success from 31 percent to 58 percent over three seasons.

## Survey Methods

Standard monitoring uses 10-minute point counts at 200-metre spacing, repeated
three times between 20 May and 30 June. Counts should start within 90 minutes of
sunrise and are not valid in winds above 20 kilometres per hour.

Observers record all birds seen or heard within a 75-metre radius. The Boreal
Bird Panel recommends at least 25 point-count stations per survey square.
